Two weeks in Denmark, two bikes, and one clockwise loop around Jutland: In the summer of 2023, Jonas and I headed north along the North Sea, crossed Skagen, and returned south on the east side. The Komoot loop was our plan. What remains, though, is not the line on the map but the wind, the dunes, a tent that stayed dry during a thunderstorm, countless shelter sites, and far too many hot dogs.

## What We Actually Rode

The 13 recorded daily stages add up to roughly **1,101 kilometres**, **5,012 metres of elevation gain**, and **52.8 hours of riding time**. The Komoot plan remains embedded below as orientation; it is not an imported reconstruction of the rides we actually completed.

## North Sea, Dunes, and the Ride to Skagen

On the west coast, Jutland was exactly what we had hoped for: lots of space, narrow roads, and dunes stretching for kilometres along the horizon. Swimming in the North Sea was one of the best moments of the trip—cold water, wide beaches, and then back onto the loaded bike.

The further north we rode, the more the wind became a constant companion. It was especially present in Skagen. The spit of land where the waves of the North and Baltic Seas meet was still a destination worth reaching. The seals we had hoped to see never showed up.

## Shelter Sites: More Than Just a Roof

The best nights were often at the simple shelter sites. We camped in a tent or inside a shelter surrounded by nature, met other travellers and locals, and found exactly the quiet we wanted after a long day on the bike.

Not every site was comfortable: the most beautiful, remote shelters often had neither water nor a toilet. Sites near car parks or towns were usually much busier. Even so, it was an excellent system for us—and noticeably more enjoyable by bicycle than by camper van. We could reach quiet places without having to hunt for a pitch.

## Headwinds, Thunderstorms, and Too Many Flats

The first stage south was the hardest of the trip: brutal headwinds, long straight sections, and the feeling that every kilometre counted twice. A heavy thunderstorm arrived later. Fortunately, the tent stayed dry—but that night made it very clear why bikepacking cannot be planned around sunny product photos alone.

Flat tyres also happened far too often. My conclusion is straightforward: on long trips, I will never ride without tubeless again. The punctures were manageable, but they cost time, nerves, and exactly the energy needed for the next stretch into the wind.

Then there was the food: sweet Danish pastries were much better than any energy bar. Hot dogs eventually were not. One is good, a few are part of the experience—after that, enough is enough.

## The East Side: More Urban, but Not Worse

The east side of Jutland felt different. It became more urban: stylish towns, more infrastructure, and less of the wild, empty nature found on the west coast. Anyone looking only for solitude will probably remember the west side more strongly.

With a little luck, though, we still found beautiful remote shelters and quiet stretches by the water in the east. That contrast defined the loop: the west coast for dunes, wind, and openness; the east side for towns, supplies, and unexpectedly quiet places to stay.

## What Remains from the Loop

The Jutland loop was not a perfectly optimised bikepacking trip. There were headwinds, thunderstorms, flats, basic infrastructure, and too many hot dogs. That is exactly why it has stayed with me so vividly.

Denmark is surprisingly straightforward for this kind of trip: you move quickly through an open landscape, find great places to sleep with a little planning, and can switch between nature and reliable supplies along the way. For us, the combination of bicycle, tent, and shelter was far more fitting than travelling by camper van.

Would I ride the loop again? Immediately—this time tubeless, with a clear limit on hot dogs.
